⚖A Note on Method, Upfront

Before the narrative: Karbala is unusual among comparative-religion topics because, unlike Adam-to-Ibrahim, it is not ancient or legendary — it is a 7th-century historical event with real chains of transmission, some very early. But the sourcing still needs to be tiered, because the tradition has three distinct layers that get blended together in popular retellings.

TierWhat it is
1. Early historical chroniclesAl-Tabari (d. 923 CE), compiling earlier material including Abu Mikhnaf's Kitab Maqtal al-Husayn (Abu Mikhnaf died ~774 CE, less than a century after the event), al-Baladhuri, and Ibn Sa'd. The closest thing to primary sources; used by Sunni and secular historians alike.
2. Later Shia devotional literature (maqtal genre)Works like Ibn Tawus (d. 1266) and the Persian Rawdat al-Shuhada of Husayn Kashefi (d. 1504), several centuries after the event. These add emotionally vivid detail that may be devotionally true and spiritually formative but is not independently attested in the earliest chronicles.
3. Independent external corroborationThe Syriac Christian chronicler Theophilus of Edessa (chief astrologer in the Abbasid court, 775–785), partially preserved in Michael the Syrian and Theophanes the Confessor, corroborates the death of Husayn and most of his men at Karbala after suffering from thirst — a non-Muslim, non-partisan source confirming the core event within a century.

Modern scholars concur that the fundamental outline of the Battle of Karbala — Husayn's refusal to pledge allegiance to Yazid, his march toward Kufa, interception by Umayyad forces, and the massacre on 10 Muharram — is historically attested and reliable, based on early reports that predate significant doctrinal elaboration, with historians such as Wilferd Madelung emphasizing the coherence of the narrative across pro-Alid and Umayyad-leaning accounts.

The compressed version, if you only remember one sentence: a 48-year-old family rivalry over who should lead the Muslim community, between two branches of the same Quraysh clan, ended with one branch's army killing the other branch's leader and his family at Karbala in 680 CE — and that single event is the central, defining trauma that separates Shia identity from Sunni identity to this day.

ⅠPart 1 — The Causes: How the Muslim Community Got Here

1.1 The succession crisis after the Prophet ﷺ

This dispute is the entire root of Sunni-Shia divergence, and it predates Karbala by decades. Sunni Islam holds that the Rashidun caliphs — Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, then Ali — were legitimate successors chosen by the community (shura, consultation). Shia Islam holds that Ali ibn Abi Talib was the Prophet's divinely designated successor from the start, based on the event of Ghadir Khumm, and that the first three caliphs usurped a position that belonged to Ali by divine appointment. This is the foundational disagreement; everything else flows from it.

1.2 Ali's caliphate and assassination (656–661 CE)

Ali finally became the fourth Rashidun caliph in 656, but his caliphate was consumed by civil war (the First Fitna) — against Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan, governor of Syria, who refused to recognize him and accused him of failing to bring Uthman's killers to justice. Ali was assassinated in 661 CE while praying in the mosque of Kufa, by a Kharijite, Ibn Muljam.

1.3 The Hasan–Mu'awiya Treaty (661 CE)

Ali's elder son Hasan ibn Ali (the second Shia Imam) was acclaimed caliph by the Kufans, but rather than prolong the civil war, he negotiated a peace settlement with Mu'awiya and stepped aside. The terms included that Mu'awiya would not appoint a successor by hereditary dynasty — a condition that would later be spectacularly broken. Hasan died in 670 CE, reportedly poisoned.

❝ From the recordTIER 1 · EARLY CHRONICLE

Shia historical accounts assert that Mu'awiya orchestrated the poisoning of Hasan by his wife Ja'da bint al-Ash'ath, motivated by the need to secure the succession for his son Yazid. This specific poisoning allegation is a Shia historical claim, not something universally accepted by Sunni historians — worth flagging since it's stated as established fact in many retellings.

1.4 Mu'awiya breaks the treaty: the hereditary turn

Before his death in 680 CE, Mu'awiya's influence prevailed, and the stability he had brought to the empire after years of political tumult following Caliph Uthman's murder allowed his son Yazid to ascend the throne, changing the nature of the future Islamic Caliphates from a semi-republican system of governance to a monarchical one.

This was the precise violation of the Hasan-Mu'awiya treaty's spirit, and it was controversial well beyond Shia circles — this act was deeply controversial and was rejected by several key figures, including Imam Husayn, Abdullah ibn Zubayr, and Abdullah ibn Umar — i.e., this wasn't only a Shia objection; prominent companions and their descendants across the early community saw hereditary succession itself as a corruption of the caliphate's original character.

1.5 Why Husayn specifically refused Yazid

When Mu'awiya died in April 680, Yazid demanded the formal oath of allegiance (bay'ah) from Husayn and other dissenters. Husayn refused, citing Yazid's moral failings — such as public indulgence in wine, gambling, and keeping hunting dogs — which he deemed incompatible with the caliphate's religious responsibilities, preferring death over legitimizing such rule.

In Medina, the Umayyad governor summoned Husayn to pledge fealty privately; Husayn refused the secrecy of it and reportedly drew his sword when pressed, then fled by night to Mecca rather than risk arrest.

☾ Theological pointTHEOLOGY

The theological core: at its core, the conflict was about who had the right to lead the Muslim community. The Shia tradition held — and holds — that leadership (imamate) belonged to the household of the Prophet (Ahl al-Bayt), beginning with Ali. The Umayyad model, by contrast, was pragmatic and political: power went to those who could hold it.

ⅡPart 2 — The Road to Karbala: Kufa, Betrayal, and the March

2.1 The letters from Kufa

While in Mecca, Husayn received letters from pro-Alids in Kufa informing him that they were tired of Umayyad rule, which they considered oppressive, and that they had no rightful leader. On certain days there would be as many as 600 letters, with messengers who enthusiastically described the support he would receive. Husayn wrote back affirmatively and sent his cousin, Muslim ibn Aqil, ahead to verify the situation.

2.2 Muslim ibn Aqil's mission and its collapse

Muslim ibn Aqil arrived in Kufa and initially secured very substantial pledges — figures cited range up to 18,000 Kufans who had pledged support. But Yazid learned of the situation and replaced the existing, comparatively lenient Kufan governor, Nu'man ibn Bashir, with Ubayd Allah ibn Ziyad — a ruthless operator who had just suppressed Basra.

As a result of Ibn Ziyad's suppression and political maneuvering, Ibn Aqil's following began to dissipate and he was forced to declare the revolt prematurely. It was defeated and Ibn Aqil was killed.

“Come home, there are enough other people here” — a Kufan mother to her son, as tribal loyalty evaporated under threat. “What will happen tomorrow when the Syrian armies start arriving from Damascus?” — a Kufan father's warning.

2.3 Husayn departs anyway

Crucially, Husayn was unaware of the change of political circumstances in Kufa and decided to depart. Abd Allah ibn Abbas and Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr advised him not to move to Iraq, or, if he was determined, not to take women and children with him. (Historians note Ibn al-Zubayr's motives in giving this advice were not entirely disinterested — he had his own claim on leadership.) Husayn departed Mecca on 8 Dhul-Hijjah 60 AH (September 680 CE) with roughly 50–72 armed companions plus family.

2.4 News of disaster reaches the caravan en route

At a place called Tha'labiyya, the small caravan received news of Ibn Aqil's execution and the indifference of the people of Kufa. Husayn at this point is reported to have considered turning back, but was persuaded to push forward by Ibn Aqil's brothers, who wanted to avenge his death — though according to Madelung and I.K.A. Howard, these specific reports are doubtful.

He informed his followers of the situation and asked them to leave. Most of the people who had joined him on the way left, while his companions from Mecca decided to stay with him. This is an important, often-underplayed detail: Husayn gave his followers a genuine, repeated opportunity to depart once the odds became clear, and the core group that remained did so knowingly.

2.5 Interception and diversion to Karbala

Husayn and his followers were intercepted by the vanguard of Yazid's army, about 1,000 men led by al-Hurr ibn Yazid al-Tamimi, south of Kufa near Qadisiyya. Hurr blocked Husayn from continuing to Kufa or returning to Medina, forcing him onto a route that ended at the desert plain of Karbala. On 2 October, the Husaynid forces entered the desert plain of Karbala, where the rest of the Umayyad force arrived by the next day.

ⅢPart 3 — The Siege: Water, Negotiation, and the Final Night

3.1 The water blockade

❝ From the recordTIER 1 · EARLY CHRONICLE

Ibn Ziyad commanded Umar ibn Sa'ad

“Prevent Husayn and his followers from accessing water and do not allow them a single drop.”

This began on 7 Muharram, three days before the battle. To force Husayn and his followers into submission, the Umayyads blocked access to the Euphrates River with 500 cavalry troops.

3.2 Failed negotiations

After negotiations with Husayn, Ibn Sa'd wrote to Ibn Ziyad that Husayn was willing to return to the Hejaz, abandoning the Kufa plan. Ibn Ziyad replied that Husayn must surrender or be subdued by force. Ibn Ziyad's demand was unconditional submission to his authority in Kufa, which Husayn would not give.

3.3 The eve of Ashura (9 Muharram)

Husayn gathers his companions, releases them from their oath of loyalty, and urges them to leave. All refuse. He spends the night in prayer, and his companions reportedly spent the night reciting Qur'an. He ordered a trench dug around the camp and filled with brushwood, to be set alight the next day to block the enemy's approach from one direction.

3.4 Al-Hurr's defection

A detail with real historical weight, not later embellishment: al-Hurr ibn Yazid al-Tamimi, the very commander who had originally blocked Husayn's path, defected to Husayn's side on the morning of the battle. Husayn's speech moved him to do so.

“Hurr, as thy mother has named thee Hurr (a free man), thou art Hurr in this world and the hereafter.” — Husayn, on Hurr's death in battle.

ⅣPart 4 — The Day of Ashura: 10 Muharram, 61 AH (10 October, 680 CE)

4.1 The battle's shape

The numerical mismatch is the most consistently reported fact across every source tier, though the exact figures vary: Husayn's retinue mustered perhaps 72 fighting men, against an Umayyad force early sources put around 4,000 and some Shia sources put far higher (up to 30,000). The lower figure (~4,000) is closer to what the earliest chronicles (Tabari, drawing on Abu Mikhnaf) support.

4.2 The fighting

Since Umayyad forces could approach Husayn's army from the front only, Ibn Sa'd ordered the tents to be burned — all except the one which Husayn and his family were using. The plan backfired and flames hindered the Umayyad advance for a while.

Husayn's companions went out to fight and fall, one after another, through the morning and early afternoon — 30 companions were martyred by the end of the morning prayers, including Zuhayr and Hurr. Then the family members joined the fight: Husayn's son Ali Akbar was killed; then Husayn's half-brothers, including Abbas, and the sons of Aqil ibn Abi Talib, Jafar ibn Abi Talib and Hasan ibn Ali were slain.

⚠ Sourcing cautionTIER 2 · LATER DEVOTIONAL

A sourcing note on Abbas's death

The account of Abbas's death is not given in the primary sources, al-Tabari and Baladhuri, but the Shia theologian Shaykh al-Mufid states in Kitab al-Irshad that Abbas went to the river together with Husayn but became separated, was surrounded, and killed. The widely circulated version — Abbas filling a waterskin for the thirsty children, having both arms severed, then killed near the riverbank — comes substantially from al-Mufid (d. 1022, a relatively early Shia jurist) and the later Ibn Tawus.

4.3 The death of Husayn

By the afternoon (after Asr prayer), only Husayn and Abbas remained. After Abbas fell, Husayn alone faced the army. Early sources record that he fought until exhausted from wounds and thirst, and was finally killed by Shimr ibn Dhi'l-Jawshan, who beheaded him. He was very thirsty and tried desperately to reach the Euphrates to drink, but an arrow struck his throat first.

⚠ Sourcing cautionTIER 2 · LATER DEVOTIONAL

On the death of Ali Asghar (the infant son)

The vivid scene of an arrow killing Husayn's infant son in his arms while seeking water is very widely told in Shia devotional tradition and is deeply embedded in Muharram recitation, but — like the Ruqayya narrative discussed in Part 5 — this level of specific detail is sourced to later maqtal literature rather than the earliest chronicles. It should be held as devotionally significant and traditionally transmitted, not as independently verified by the earliest historical record in the same way the broader battle is.

4.4 After the killing

It was close to sunset when the head of the Imam was given to Khuli so that he could take it to Ibn Ziyad. Umar ibn Sa'd then ordered his soldiers to trample the body with horses.

4.5 The burial

According to some reports, a group of people from the Banu Asad tribe living near Karbala went to the battlefield at night and buried the bodies, on 11 or 13 Muharram, 61 AH. Even the casualty count is disputed across sources: the majority of historians report seventy-two; al-Mas'udi states eighty-seven.

ⅤPart 5 — The Aftermath: Captivity, Kufa, and Yazid's Court in Damascus

5.1 The captives

The surviving women and children — led practically by Zaynab bint Ali (Husayn's sister) and nominally headed, as the only surviving adult male, by the gravely ill Ali ibn Husayn Zayn al-Abidin (later the 4th Shia Imam, who survived precisely because his illness kept him from the fighting) — were taken captive.

The captives were marched back to Kufa, arriving there on 12 Muharram. There are reports that the women were dishonored and ogled along the way, and that the captives were humiliated, carried on unsaddled camels, and, according to al-Tabari, bound in ropes and shackles. They were then paraded in shackles and unveiled around the city alongside the heads of Husayn and his companions on spears.

5.2 Ubayd Allah ibn Ziyad's court in Kufa

In Kufa, Ibn Ziyad nearly had Ali Zayn al-Abidin executed: Ibn Ziyad ordered the execution of Ali ibn Husayn but was dissuaded when Zaynab protected her nephew and asked to be killed before him, as reported by the early historians Abu Mikhnaf, Ibn Sa'd, and al-Tabari. This is reported in genuinely early sources, so it carries real historical weight, not just devotional weight.

5.3 The journey and arrival in Damascus

After releasing the lesser captives, Ibn Ziyad imprisoned the Hashimite captives for a while and then sent them to Damascus. The journey was roughly 1,100 km and took several weeks under harsh conditions.

5.4 In Yazid's court

This is the part of the story that proved most damaging to Yazid's reputation even among later Sunni historians, and it's well attested: in the presence of the captives, Yazid had put the head of Imam al-Husayn in a gold tub and was hitting it with his staff.

⚠ Sourcing cautionTIER 2 · LATER DEVOTIONAL

One later report (from Imam al-Rida, a Shia source several generations removed) adds that Yazid played chess and drank over the tub containing the head — this specific elaboration sits in the devotional tier, though the basic act of striking the severed head with a staff is more broadly reported and provoked contemporary objection: some people present opposed Yazid's behavior, including Yahya b. al-Hakam, whom Yazid punched, and Abu Barza al-Aslami, whom Yazid ordered removed from the palace.

5.5 Zaynab's sermon and Ali Zayn al-Abidin's sermon

This is one of the best-attested and most consequential parts of the aftermath. Ibn Tayfur (a 9th-century, relatively early, cross-sectarian source) records two speeches about Karbala in his Balaghat al-nisa', one attributed to Umm Kulthum in the market of Kufa, the other ascribed to Zaynab in the court of Yazid in Damascus. Most Shia authors later attributed both sermons to Zaynab.

“O Yazid! Do you think that we have become humble and despicable owing to the martyrdom of our people and our own captivity?... You have, however, forgotten what Allah says: ‘The disbelievers must not think that our respite is for their good; We only give them time to let them increase their sins. For them there will be a humiliating torment.’” (Qur'an 3:178)

“By Allah, I see only beauty in what has occurred. Those martyred were destined to fall for a righteous cause — and their sacrifice will rouse this sleeping ummah.”

Even within Shia tradition's own account of Yazid's reaction, there's a more complicated picture than simple villainy: some early reports indicate Yazid prodded the head in a gesture of vindication before expressing dismay at the extent of the bloodshed, reportedly stating he had not ordered the killing of Husayn's family and blaming Ibn Ziyad for overstepping. This does not exonerate Yazid — he ordered the suppression and demanded submission under threat of force — but it complicates a simple “Yazid personally wanted Husayn's family slaughtered” reading.

5.6 A genuinely contested detail worth flagging directly: Ruqayya

⚠ Sourcing cautionTIER 2 · LATER DEVOTIONAL

This matters because it's so often treated as settled fact

The historicity of Ruqayya bint Husayn as a distinct daughter of Husayn who accompanied him to Karbala and died in captivity in Damascus is contested among scholars, primarily due to the absence of references in early Islamic historical accounts. Primary sources — Abu Mikhnaf's maqtal and al-Tabari — enumerate Husayn's female companions (Zaynab, Sukayna, Fatima) but make no mention of a young girl named Ruqayya or any child dying from grief upon seeing her father's severed head. The narrative emerges in later Shia devotional literature; the Damascus shrine traditionally associated with her dates structurally to 1133 CE, with no inscriptions linking it to Husayn's era.

5.7 Release and return

Yazid allowed the captives to mourn for some days in Damascus. Umayyad women, including Hind, Yazid's wife, joined the Ahl al-Bayt in the ruin where the captives were held; they kissed their hands and feet and mourned for three days. Finally, the captives were returned to Medina with respect and reverence. Zaynab died not long after, in 682 CE, having borne the weight of telling the story to the wider Muslim world — which is exactly why Shia tradition calls her Umm al-Masa'ib, “mother of calamities.”

ⅥPart 6 — Political Aftermath: How This Reshaped the Muslim World

  • The Tawwabin (“Penitents”) uprising, 685 CE: Led by Sulayman ibn Surad, Kufans wracked with guilt marched under the slogan “Revenge for Husayn.” Though 16,000 enlisted, only 4,000 mustered. They met the Umayyads in January 685 at the three-day Battle of Ayn al-Warda; most of the Tawwabin, including Ibn Surad, were killed.
  • Mukhtar al-Thaqafi's revolt: Following Husayn's martyrdom, Mukhtar positioned himself as an avenger amid the power vacuum after Yazid's death in November 683. He seized Kufa and hunted down many directly responsible for Karbala, including Umar ibn Sa'd and Shimr.
  • The wider Second Fitna: the Battle of Karbala began the Second Fitnah. By crushing Husayn at the start, the Umayyad dynasty destroyed a powerful rival to their power — but it also martyred Husayn, leading to a split between Shia and Sunni Islam. Mecca fell to Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr, who claimed the caliphate, while Kharijite forces also rose in revolt.
  • Long-term consequence: though the battle was one-sided and ended in a decisive Umayyad victory, the fallen soldiers have ever since been venerated as martyrs of Islam, and the event became one of the core reasons for opposition against the Umayyads, who were overthrown around 70 years later (the Abbasid Revolution, 750 CE).
  • Abbasid-era suppression: even decades later, Karbala remained politically dangerous — the Abbasid caliph al-Mutawakkil (r. 847–861) demolished the shrine of Husayn and punished pilgrimage with death, fearing it could ignite “another Karbala.”

ⅦPart 7 — Shia Theological Significance: The Imamate and Why Karbala Is Central

7.1 The doctrine of Imamate

To understand why Karbala carries the weight it does for Shia Muslims, you need the underlying doctrine, not just the story. The Imamate is a fundamental belief in Shia Islam, based on the concept that God would not leave humanity without access to divine guidance. According to the Twelvers, an Imam of the Age is always the divinely appointed authority on all matters of faith and law. Ali was the first Imam, followed by the male descendants of Muhammad through his daughter Fatimah. Each Imam was the son of the previous Imam, with the exception of Husayn, who was the brother of Hasan.

☾ Theological pointTHEOLOGY

The metaphysical claim

In Twelver theology, the Imam is not merely a political leader but a metaphysical necessity and the “Proof of God” (Hujjat-Allah) on Earth — the world cannot exist for a single moment without a divinely appointed authority to provide spiritual guidance and prevent corruption of the divine message. Importantly: the Shia Imams are seen as infallible, though they are explicitly not prophets (nabi) nor messengers (rasul) — they carry out Muhammad's message rather than receive new revelation.

The Twelve Imams, in order:

  • 1. Ali ibn Abi Talib — 2. Hasan ibn Ali — 3. Husayn ibn Ali
  • 4. Ali Zayn al-Abidin — 5. Muhammad al-Baqir — 6. Ja'far al-Sadiq
  • 7. Musa al-Kadhim — 8. Ali al-Rida — 9. Muhammad al-Taqi
  • 10. Ali al-Hadi — 11. Hasan al-Askari — 12. Muhammad al-Mahdi (in occultation since 868 CE; believed by Twelvers to remain alive, to return at the end of time)

7.2 Why Husayn's death specifically is “the most decisive event”

The martyrdom of the third imam, Husayn, during the second civil war in 680 is the most decisive event in Shi'ite history. The theological reasoning: Husayn, as the divinely designated Imam of his time, was not merely a political claimant defeated in battle — he was, in this framework, God's appointed proof on earth, killed unjustly by a usurping, illegitimate power. That makes Karbala not just a tragedy but a paradigmatic confrontation between divinely sanctioned truth and worldly tyranny — what scholars studying Shia ritual life call the “Karbala Paradigm.”

Anthropologist Michael Fischer states that commemoration of Karbala presents Shia with “life models and norms of behavior” applicable to all aspects of life. According to Olmo Gölz, the Karbala Paradigm provides heroic norms and a martyr ethos, representing an embodiment of the battle between good and evil, justice and injustice.

7.3 Intercession theology

This is a specific doctrinal claim worth being precise about, since it's a real point of difference with Sunni theology: the 17th-century Safavid-era theologian Majlisi linked Husayn's martyrdom at Karbala with the Imam's power to grant intercession in paradise to those who honor him through ritual commemoration. Shias believe that mourning Husayn is not merely remembering history but a sacred duty that earns spiritual merit — and that pilgrimage to his shrine, like weeping, wipes out sins to a great extent.

A. The Combined Family Tree: Banu Hashim and Banu Umayya

How to read this tree

  • Abd Manaf ibn Qusayy is the shared ancestor. His son Hashim founded Banu Hashim (the Prophet's own clan); his other son Abd Shams founded the line that becomes Banu Umayya, through Abd Shams's grandson Umayya.
  • On the Hashim side: Abd al-Muttalib had two sons relevant here — Abdullah (father of the Prophet Muhammad) and Abu Talib (father of Ali). This makes Muhammad and Ali first cousins, and Ali later became the Prophet's son-in-law by marrying Fatimah.
  • On the Abd Shams side: the line runs Umayya → Harb → Abu Sufyan → Mu'awiya I → Yazid I. Abu Sufyan was the Quraysh's chief military and political opponent of the Prophet for most of his life, only accepting Islam after the conquest of Mecca in 630 CE.
  • The two branches had already been rivals for generations before Islam, competing for prestige and leadership within the Quraysh of Mecca. Islam did not create this rivalry — it inherited it, and then the succession crisis after the Prophet's death gave the old rivalry a new, much higher-stakes battlefield: who has the right to lead the entire Muslim community.

☾ Theological pointTHEOLOGY

Why Husayn breaks the father-to-son pattern

Every Imam is the direct son of the one before him, with exactly one exception — Husayn succeeded his brother Hasan, not his father, because Hasan died without his own succession being passed to a son in that moment; leadership passed sideways to his younger brother instead. This is the only branch-point in the entire twelve-link chain.

A note on the Mahdi and Karbala's unfinished business, theologically speaking: Many Shia traditions tie the eventual return of the 12th Imam to a final, ultimate reckoning for the injustice of Karbala — the Mahdi's mission is described as bringing perfect justice to the world, which in the devotional imagination directly answers the injustice done to Husayn. This is theological framing, not something to treat as established historical prediction.

C. Yazid I — Lineage and Background

Full name and direct line

Yazid ibn Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan ibn Harb ibn Umayya. Born c. 645–647 CE in Syria; died 11 November 683 CE in Damascus, after ruling roughly three and a half years.

How he came to power

Mu'awiya I nominated Yazid as his heir in 676 CE (56 AH) — a decision opposed by several senior Muslim figures from the Hejaz, including Husayn and Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr. This was the first hereditary succession to the caliphate in Islamic history; previous transitions had involved some form of nomination-plus-acclamation by senior Muslims, not father-to-son inheritance as a fixed rule. When Mu'awiya died in April 680, Yazid demanded the oath of allegiance (bay'ah) from Husayn and other dissenters, ordering the governor of Medina: “force al-Husayn to give allegiance and if he refuses, send me his head.”

What he actually did during his reign (beyond Karbala)

  • Ordered the suppression of Husayn's movement, resulting in the killing at Karbala in 680, carried out under his governor Ubayd Allah ibn Ziyad.
  • Battle of al-Harra, August 683: when Medina renounced its allegiance to him, Yazid sent a 12,000-strong army that defeated the city's defenders and sacked Medina, the Prophet's own city — an act that shocked even many who were not Husayn's partisans.
  • Siege of Mecca, 683: shortly after, his forces besieged Mecca (where Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr had declared a rival claim to the caliphate), reportedly damaging the Kaaba with catapult fire before the siege was lifted when Yazid died.
  • Continued his father's centralized, Syria-based administrative model; strengthened Syria's military defenses; reformed parts of the financial and irrigation systems around Damascus.
⚖ Range of opinion

How he is judged — and the real range of opinion, even within Sunni Islam

It would be a mistake to flatten this into “all Muslims agree Yazid was evil” or “it's only a Shia view.” The 12th-century scholar Ibn al-Salah described Sunni opinion as split into three real camps: one group that defends him as a legitimate ruler, one that curses and condemns him, and a moderate middle group that neither venerates nor curses him, treating him like any other historical king rather than placing him with the rightly-guided caliphs.

Adding real complexity rather than resolving it: the influential historian Ibn Kathir stated that the entire collection of hadith reports specifically condemning Yazid by name are weak or outright fabricated, even though Ibn Kathir did not dispute the basic facts of Karbala itself. Other scholars, including Ahmad ibn Hanbal (founder of one of the four Sunni schools of law), are reported to have held that Yazid should be cursed, citing the Qur'anic condemnation of those who spread corruption and bloodshed.

Modern secular historians tend to land in between the polemical extremes: they generally view him as a capable administrator who continued his father's policies competently, while also holding him directly responsible for the political decisions — the water blockade, the order to subdue or kill Husayn, the sack of Medina, the siege of Mecca — that made him one of the most reviled rulers in Islamic memory regardless of sect.

D. The Umayyad Dynasty — Lineage and Background

Origin of the name and the clan

The Umayyads, or Banu Umayya, were a prominent clan of the Quraysh tribe of Mecca, descended from Umayya ibn Abd Shams. Abd Shams was a son of Abd Manaf ibn Qusayy — the same great-great-grandfather shared with the Prophet's own clan, Banu Hashim, founded by Abd Shams's brother Hashim. According to tradition, the Umayyad family and Muhammad both descended from this common ancestor, and they are originally from the city of Mecca — meaning the Umayyad-Hashimite rivalry was, from its origin, a rivalry between two branches of one extended family, not between outsiders and insiders.

Pre-Islamic standing

Umayya ibn Abd Shams was a respected pre-Islamic leader known for his wealth and trade influence in Mecca. His descendants, including Harb and Abu Sufyan, remained powerful Quraysh nobility through the rise of Islam — and largely on the opposing side. Abu Sufyan led Quraysh resistance to Muhammad for nearly two decades (including command at the Battle of Uhud, where Muslims suffered a serious defeat) before submitting to Islam at the conquest of Mecca in 630 CE.

How the dynasty actually started ruling

The Umayyad family had first come to real power under the third Rashidun caliph, Uthman ibn Affan (r. 644–656), himself an early companion of Muhammad from the Umayyad clan, who appointed several Umayyad relatives to governorships. The dynasty proper, however, was founded by Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan, the long-serving governor of Syria, who refused to recognize Ali as caliph after Uthman's assassination, fought the First Fitna against him, and after Ali's death and Hasan's abdication, became the first Umayyad caliph in 661 CE, with Damascus as his capital — a deliberate shift away from Medina and the Hejaz, the heartland of the old Muslim elite and the Prophet's own family.

What the dynasty is remembered for, beyond Karbala

  • Transforming the caliphate from a system based on consultation and selection among senior Muslims (shura) into a hereditary monarchy — a structural change many contemporaries, not only Shia, regarded as a genuine corruption of the earlier model.
  • Massive territorial expansion: at its height under the Marwanids, the Umayyad Caliphate stretched from the Iberian Peninsula (Spain/Portugal) to the Indus Valley in South Asia, one of the largest empires in history to that point.
  • Making Arabic the administrative language across the empire and establishing strong centralized bureaucratic control, while relying heavily on existing local administrators in conquered Byzantine and Persian territories.
  • A reputation, fairly or not, for favoring Arab Muslims over non-Arab converts, which many later Muslims (and the Abbasid revolutionaries who eventually overthrew them in 750 CE) saw as a betrayal of Islam's stated principle of equality among believers regardless of ethnicity.
  • Surviving the dynasty's overthrow: one Umayyad prince, Abd al-Rahman I, escaped the Abbasid purge and fled to Spain, founding a new Umayyad emirate (later caliphate) in al-Andalus that lasted until 1031 — meaning the family line itself outlasted the original caliphate by centuries, just relocated to Europe.

ⅧPart 8 — Ritual Practice: How Karbala Is Commemorated

8.1 Muharram and Ashura — the basic structure

Mourning rituals take place during the first ten days of Muharram, culminating with processions in major Shia cities on the tenth day, Ashura. The main component of mourning ceremonies is the recitation of Karbala narratives intended to raise the audience's sympathy and move them to tears. These gatherings (majalis, singular majlis) typically happen in a dedicated building called a Husayniya (also imambarah, takiya).

8.2 Matam (chest-beating)

Mild forms — striking one's face and chest in grief (latm, sina-zani, matam) — are common today, intended to share in the pain of Husayn.

8.3 Tatbir (extreme self-flagellation) — genuinely controversial, even within Shia Islam

⚠ Sourcing cautionTIER 2 · LATER DEVOTIONAL

Not the Shia mainstream position

Tatbir, also called Zanjeer Zani or Qama Zani, involves striking oneself with chains or swords. It has been considered haram (“forbidden”) by some Shia clergy, who cite it as self-harm. It traces to the 19th century in Iran, introduced by Qizilbash tribes — a practice borrowed from Central Asian mourning customs, not an original Islamic practice. Iranian Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei has banned the practice in Iran since 1994. Both Iraqi cleric Hussein Al-Sadr and Hezbollah's Hassan Nasrallah have promoted blood donation over the practice instead.

8.4 Ta'ziyeh (passion plays)

Ta'ziya or shabih-khwani is the dramatic reenactment of Karbala narratives, practiced today in Iran, in Shia communities of the western Gulf shore, and in Lebanon.

8.5 Arba'een — the 40-day pilgrimage

Forty days after Ashura comes Arba'een, marked by a massive pilgrimage on foot to Husayn's shrine in Karbala — historically up to one million pilgrims annually; modern Arba'een walks now draw far larger numbers, often cited in the tens of millions, making it one of the largest annual gatherings of human beings on Earth.

8.6 The turbah (prayer clay)

Twelvers often use a piece of clay (turbah), typically from Karbala, during prostration in prayer, signifying humility and connection to sacred history.

ⅨPart 9 — The Sunni Perspective: Shared Grief, Different Theology

It's important to have this clearly, since the topic can get flattened into “Shia mourn it, Sunnis don't care” — that's not accurate.

What Sunnis and Shia agree on

Sunni Muslims likewise regard the incident as a historical tragedy; Husayn and his companions are widely regarded as martyrs by both Sunni and Shia Muslims. Many Sunnis regard the killing of Husayn at Karbala as a tragic event in Islamic history and commemorate it through sermons, remembrance, or reflection — though these observances generally differ from Shia mourning rituals.

Where the practice diverges

Sunni Muslims observe Ashura as a day when fasting is not officially required but is considered desirable, since the Prophet himself fasted on Ashura. Sunni observance is tied primarily to older, pre-Karbala associations — according to Sunni tradition, Ashura marks the parting of the Red Sea, Noah's disembarkation from the Ark, God's forgiveness of Adam, and Joseph's/Yousaf release from prison.

This means Sunni and Shia Muslims observe the same calendar date for substantially different primary reasons — Sunnis primarily for these older prophetic anniversaries (plus reflection on Husayn), Shia primarily and overwhelmingly for Husayn's martyrdom.

A theological objection some Sunni scholars raise

Ibn Taymiyya rejects both mourning and celebrating on Ashura because, he contends, neither was practiced by Muhammad; he does, however, encourage fasting to emulate Muhammad. Note: this is one influential scholar's specific legal position within Sunni Islam, not a universal Sunni consensus — other Sunni scholars across history have expressed grief over Karbala without endorsing or opposing organized Shia ritual mourning specifically.

On Yazid specifically

It's worth being precise that opposing Yazid's actions at Karbala is not a uniquely Shia position — mainstream Sunni scholarship across history has been broadly critical of Yazid's character and his role in Husayn's death, even while Sunni and Shia theology differ sharply on the doctrinal significance of Husayn's death (martyred Imam with intercessory power vs. martyred grandson of the Prophet and righteous victim of tyranny, without the Imamate framework attached).
